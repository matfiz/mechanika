Lecture 3

Hamilton’s principle and Noether’s theorem

Lecture 2 derived Lagrange’s equations from Newton’s law with ideal constraints. This lecture asks what single principle gives them, and why a symmetry gives a conserved quantity. The answer is that they are the conditions for one number, the action of a path, to be stationary. That is the course’s first answer to its question: a system is a configuration space with a Lagrangian, and a motion is a curve that makes the action stationary with fixed ends. The principle explains why the equations keep their form under changes of coordinates and why the Lagrangian is not unique. Momentum appears in the boundary term of the variation. Noether’s theorem then associates a conserved quantity with each quasi-symmetry. The lecture closes with the energy function and the central force, whose motion is planar because angular momentum is conserved.

3.3 Coordinate invariance and gauge freedom

Covariance

Newton’s law mx¨=Fm\ddot x=Fmx¨=F changes its form in curvilinear coordinates: in polar coordinates a centrifugal term appears (Example 2.12). The Euler–Lagrange equations do not change their form. This property is called covariance, and the reason for it is that the action does not refer to coordinates.

Summary

  • The first answer: a system is a configuration space with a Lagrangian, and its motions are the paths that make the action stationary with fixed ends (Theorem 3.4) — stationary, not least (Proposition 3.5).
