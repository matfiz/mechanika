Lecture 10

Momentum maps and reduction

Separating the central force in Lecture 9 produced the constants L3L_3L3​ and ∣L∣2\abs{\Lvec}^{2}∣L∣2, and they came from the rotations. This lecture turns a whole group of canonical symmetries into conserved quantities. The momentum map collects observables whose flows generate the group action. For lifted transformations of positions, its components are Noether charges p⋅ξp\cdot\xip⋅ξ. For rotations it is L=q×p\Lvec=q\times pL=q×p. Conserved quantities then reduce the motion. A cyclic variable removes a degree of freedom, and the central force reduces to one degree of freedom. Kepler’s problem has an additional conserved vector that is not the charge of any point symmetry. It explains why bounded orbits with nonzero angular momentum close.

Summary

  • Symmetry and momentum: a symplectic action of a matrix group, its generators (Lemma 10.4) and its momentum map (Definition 10.5). A lifted action has Jξ=p⋅ξU(q)\momap_\xi=p\cdot\xi_U(q)Jξ​=p⋅ξU​(q) (Theorem 10.6), and rotations have J=L=q×p\momap=\Lvec=q\times pJ=L=q×p (Example 10.8). An invariant HHH conserves every component of J\momapJ (Theorem 10.9).
  • Reduction: a cyclic variable reduces by one degree of freedom (Theorem 10.10), and the central force with L≠0\Lvec\ne0L=0 reduces to a motion in VeffV_{\mathrm{eff}}Veff​ (Theorem 10.12).
  • Kepler: the vector A\LRLA closes every bounded orbit with L≠0\Lvec\ne0L=0 (Proposition 10.14), though no point symmetry produces it (Proposition 10.15).
  • Next: the rigid body has the rotations as its symmetry too. Dividing them out completely leaves a bracket that is degenerate, and Lecture 11 studies such brackets.
